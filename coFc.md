# What Is a Digital Chain of Custody?

Knowing where an asset is can be useful.

Knowing **where it has been, when it moved, what happened during the journey and what conditions it experienced** can be much more valuable.

That is the idea behind a digital chain of custody.

A traditional GPS tracker is usually designed to answer a simple question: **where is this asset now?** A digital chain-of-custody platform goes further by bringing together location history, timestamps, sensor readings and operational events into a single, auditable record.

For organisations responsible for important, sensitive or high-value assets, that wider history can make it much easier to understand what happened during an asset's journey without relying only on phone calls, messages or individual recollection.

## What does "digital chain of custody" mean?

A digital chain of custody is a chronological record of an asset's journey and the events associated with it.

That record can include information such as:

- GPS location and historical routes
- timestamps
- movement events
- box or enclosure-open events
- temperature
- humidity
- free-fall or impact events
- immutable system logs

The exact data captured depends on the device and deployment, but the principle is the same: instead of looking at individual pieces of information in isolation, the platform connects them into an operational history.

This creates a single place where authorised users can review what the system recorded over time.

## GPS tracking and digital chain of custody are not the same thing

GPS tracking is an important part of a digital chain of custody, but it is only one part.

Imagine that you are responsible for a sensitive asset travelling between two locations.

A GPS tracker may tell you that the asset is currently at its destination.

That is useful, but it leaves a number of questions unanswered:

- What route did it take?
- What time did it leave?
- When did it arrive?
- Was it moved after arrival?
- Was its enclosure opened during the journey?
- Did it experience a sudden drop or impact?
- Was it exposed to unusual temperature or humidity?

If those questions matter to your operation, a latest-position marker on a map is not enough.

A digital chain of custody is designed to preserve the wider context around the journey.

## Why an auditable history matters

Many organisations still manage asset movements through a mixture of phone calls, spreadsheets, messaging apps and verbal updates.

That can work perfectly well when everything goes to plan.

The difficulty appears when someone later needs to understand exactly what happened.

One person may remember an asset arriving at 14:30. Another may have a WhatsApp message sent at 14:42. A spreadsheet may have been updated later in the afternoon. The driver may remember taking a different route.

None of those sources is necessarily wrong, but they do not create a single operational record.

A digital chain-of-custody platform brings the information recorded by the tracking system into one place, creating an auditable timeline that can be reviewed later.

The value is not in claiming that the system can prove every real-world event. It is in creating a consistent record of the locations, sensor readings and device events captured during the operation.

## What information can form part of a digital chain of custody?

Location is usually the starting point, but it becomes much more useful when combined with other data.

### Location and historical routes

Live location helps an operations team understand where an asset is now.

Historical routes make it possible to look back at the journey afterwards and review where the asset travelled over a particular period.

This can be especially useful when the expected route and the actual route are different, or when an operational team needs to understand a delay.

### Timestamps

Events become significantly more useful when they are tied to time.

A record that an asset moved is helpful. A record showing that it moved at 11:43, arrived at another location at 12:18 and was opened at 12:26 provides much more operational context.

### Movement events

A tracking device can record when an asset begins moving or when movement occurs after it was expected to remain stationary.

For some organisations, that may be more important than constantly watching a map.

### Box-open events

Where the hardware supports it, an enclosure-open event can record that a box, case or container was opened.

This does not explain why it was opened or who opened it, but it adds another useful event to the asset's recorded history.

### Temperature and humidity

Some assets are sensitive to their environment.

Temperature and humidity monitoring can help organisations understand the conditions an asset experienced during storage or transport rather than relying only on conditions measured at the start or end of a journey.

### Free-fall and impact events

A free-fall event can indicate that an asset or its tracking device experienced a sudden drop.

Again, the event itself does not tell the entire story, but it can provide useful context when reviewing the journey of fragile or important equipment.

## Why immutable logs are important

An audit trail is only useful if the history of the system can be trusted as a record of what the platform captured.

ATLAS uses immutable logs so that recorded events form a persistent operational history.

For authorised users, that means there is a consistent timeline available for later review rather than a collection of separate updates that may have been edited, misplaced or remembered differently.

This is one of the key differences between simply receiving tracking updates and maintaining a digital chain of custody.

## What happens when mobile coverage is poor?

Any connected tracking system has to deal with the realities of mobile coverage.

A tracker may move through rural areas, large buildings, basements or other places where one mobile network has limited or no signal.

ATLAS devices use multi-network SIM cards so they can connect across multiple available mobile networks rather than depending on a single network provider.

That does not mean mobile dead zones disappear entirely. No cellular tracking system can guarantee continuous coverage everywhere.

Instead, multi-network connectivity can improve the opportunity for the device to remain connected as it moves between areas with different network coverage. Where a device is configured to retain data during a temporary loss of connectivity, recorded information can be transmitted when a connection becomes available again.

The result is a more resilient approach than relying on one network alone.

## Digital chain of custody does not replace physical procedures

A digital chain of custody should not be confused with the physical procedures an organisation uses to control access to an asset.

Physical seals, staff responsibilities, authorised handovers, secure storage, sign-off procedures and organisational policies may all remain important.

The digital platform supports those procedures by providing an auditable record of the information captured by the tracking system.

It can help an organisation answer questions about an asset's recorded journey, but it does not replace good operational controls.

The strongest deployments usually combine both.

## Where can digital chain of custody be useful?

Digital chain of custody can be useful anywhere the history of an asset matters as much as its current location.

Examples may include:

- high-value equipment moving between sites
- sensitive public-sector assets
- temperature-sensitive goods
- remote or temporary equipment
- secure deliveries
- reusable transport cases or containers
- election equipment and ballot-box movements
- assets where unexpected movement or opening needs to be reviewed

It is not necessary for every tracking use case.

If all you need to know is roughly where a low-risk asset is, a conventional GPS tracker may be enough.

Digital chain of custody becomes more relevant when someone may later need to ask:

**What happened to this asset during its journey?**

## A real-world example: ballot-box tracking

During the 2024 UK Parliamentary election, ATLAS was used to support the tracking of ballot boxes for Plymouth City Council.

Election logistics are a good example of why an operational history can matter.

Teams are coordinating time-sensitive movements across multiple locations, with many people involved and very little room for confusion.

In that environment, a tracking platform can give authorised users visibility of asset movements and provide a historical record that can be reviewed afterwards.

Elections are only one example. The same underlying principle applies in commercial, industrial and public-sector operations: when an asset matters, the ability to review its journey can be valuable.

## From "where is it?" to "what happened?"

The simplest way to understand digital chain of custody is to compare the questions being asked.

A basic tracker answers:

**Where is it?**

A digital chain-of-custody platform can help answer:

**Where has it been? When did it move? What events were recorded? What conditions did it experience?**

That difference changes tracking from a live-location tool into an operational record.

For many organisations, that historical context is where much of the value lies.
